Before the first chapter, an accounting

Of a million voices, and how few of them say anything

A large number at the top of a page is a kind of boast, so here is what the large number actually contains. Most of a million people at a deathbed are not talking about what life is for. They are saying they are sorry. They are telling a stranger to be strong. They are making a joke because the alternative is unbearable. Roughly four in five never touch the question at all, and that is not a failure of theirs. Nobody asked them.

So the million is a quarry and not a monument. Everything that follows was cut out of it by hand, a little at a time, and most of the stone is still in the ground.

The collapse from a million to a few thousand is not waste. It is what honesty costs. Every step down is a place where a shortcut would have produced a confident number and a false one.
The unread remainder is not lost. It is waiting, and the count of what has been read closely goes up every time the work continues.
Here begins the first chapter

IOf the places, and what each one answers

Ask a dying man's comment section what life is for, and the answer has an accent. Among the people who name something, this is how often the thing they name is God. The order was not decided in advance. It fell out of the text, and it happens to follow how devout each place actually is, which is the only reason to trust it.

Japanese, after a terminal diagnosis

Because death comes one day, I make sure to talk and laugh with my children, every day.

Both of them are looking straight at death. One sees God in it. The other sees a child at the kitchen table. Nothing in the method put them that way round.

Trust the two ends of that table and distrust its middle. When the same eight tongues are compared inside a single situation, so that grief is only ever set beside grief, the top and the bottom hold firm. The order in between comes apart. Korean speakers are overwhelmingly religious beside an account of near death and barely religious at a widow's side. It is the same language in the same month of the same year. So a tongue does not really have a number. It has a number that depends on what life has just done to it, and the six tongues in the middle of this table sit inside one another's swing.

7.4×Arabic against Japanese in a widow's grief, with the situation held still. 2.6× beside a near death. The gap at the ends is real either way.
Nine languages here rest on fragments that a slower machine judged one at a time. Ten more are gathered and being held back, because the quick way of counting them turned out to miss any faith written in the wrong alphabet.
A tongue is not a country, and not a faith. It is only who happened to be typing. Read the ends of the table as a rumour that keeps turning out to be true, and read the middle as not yet known.
The second chapter

IIThat God is not one thing

Left alone to sort themselves, the fragments refused to let religion stay a single category. It came apart along the seams of tradition. So a place does not simply hold more faith or less of it. It holds a different kind, and the single number in the chapter before this one was hiding that.

Four kinds surfaced without being asked for: the hereafter, submission, surrender, and thanksgiving.
Arabic and Indonesian gather at the first two. The Catholic tongues gather at the last two. No person drew that line. A machine grouping sentences by their sense drew it.
Directional only. The grouping is coarse, and how many groups to look for was a choice rather than a discovery.
The third chapter

IIIOf Korea, which ruins the easy answer

Japan came back the least religious place in this book, and the obvious objection is that Japan is simply modern, and rich, and Asian, and that such places stop talking about God. Korea is next door. It is just as modern and just as rich.

47%Korean, of God
11%Japanese, of God

Nearly five times apart. So it is not Asia, and it is not wealth, and it is not the century. Whatever is happening in Japan is happening in Japan.

This is the entire use of a control. It does not prove what Japan is. It removes the three explanations you would have reached for first.
Korea's figure comes from the quick pass, not the careful one. The gap is far too wide to be an artifact of that, but the decimal should not be trusted.
The fourth chapter

IVWhat will not be mixed with anything

People who find life meaningful for worldly reasons tend to hand you several at once. My work, and my children, and who I am becoming. They stack. But when a person says God, they say almost nothing else.

Measured against chance, every worldly pair leans together and every pairing with God leans apart. Freedom and God almost never arrive in the same breath. Becoming oneself and God, barely. Even service to others, which sounds like the natural companion of faith, sits below what chance alone would produce.

Faith arrives as a whole answer. Everything else arrives as a mixture.

Indonesian, a widow, to herself
Aku spesial. Aku berharga. Aku dicintai.
I am special. I am worth something. I am loved.
English, leaving the church
I left Christianity and I have never felt any better.
0.1×how often freedom and God are named together, set against chance.
3.3×how often craft and service are, for comparison.
Read out of 2,758 statements that named at least one thing, each judged individually.
Some pairs rest on very few statements. The pattern holds across the well-populated ones too, which is the only reason it is in the book.
The fifth chapter

VOf rooms, and how each one changes the answer

The same question, in the same language, asked in three different rooms. Beneath a hospice video, where saying it costs you something. Among engineers thinking out loud. In an open argument where everyone is keeping score. Choose a room, and watch what life is for rearrange itself.

Confession reaches for family and God. Reflection reaches for the work of one's hands. Argument reaches for philosophy.
The cleanest comparison is the first two rooms, where the crowds are both wide and the only real difference is what it costs to speak.
The arguing room leans religious partly because faith forums were among the places sampled. Read that column lightly.
The sixth chapter

VINineteen years, and the thing that arrived in them

One of the four sources keeps its dates, so the same question can be put to the same crowd every year from 2007 to 2026. Two events walk into the picture without being invited. A plague, and a machine that learned to answer back.

For fifteen years, machines had nothing to do with what life was for. Then, in the year after the machine learned to answer back, one comment in seven about meaning or exhaustion or purpose mentions it.

0.4 to 14.4%talk of machines, across the whole span.
Death doubles in 2020, holds through 2021, then falls back. Nobody told the method that a pandemic had happened.
Talk of meaninglessness roughly triples, with no single event behind it.
Counted by word rather than by sense, on a crowd already selected for talking about this at all. The shapes are sturdy. The heights are not.
The seventh chapter

VIIOf how people hold on when nothing will improve

Every chapter before this one asks what makes a life worth living. This one asks something harder, and the book turns out to be better suited to it. A third of everything gathered here comes from people whose situation was not going to get better: a diagnosis with a date on it, a bed being sat beside, a marriage of fifty years ending in a room. Nobody asked them how they were managing. Here is what they reached for anyway.

Read the fourth row against the fifth. Every staged account of grief treats acceptance as the place you are travelling towards. Here, keeping the dead present is at least as common as accepting that they are gone, and may well be more common, though on this sample the two cannot be told apart with confidence, which is why both rows are hatched. The stronger claim is the simpler one: a way of holding on that ranks alongside acceptance is entirely missing from the standard instrument. It had to be added, because the fragments kept insisting on it.

English, widowed, three and a half years on
I refuse to accept that our love is over. It goes on. I will never give up on her.
English, a mother, one year on
I buy little trinkets for my fairy angel and put them in her memory box. I sketch her. I talk to her.
English, widowed
The cruelest words a person could say to us is move on, or get over it. The love and the memories keep them alive.

That is not denial. Every one of them knows exactly what has happened. They have simply declined the instruction to stop, and made the refusal into a way of standing up.

The second row deserves a note of its own. Saying it out loud, to strangers, is almost as common as God. Which means a quarter of the coping in this book is the act that produced this book. The corpus is made of the thing it is measuring.

311,298comments from situations that were not going to improve. Of 2,400 read closely, 46% describe a way of holding on.
The religious gradient from the first chapter appears again here, through a different question, a different instrument and a different vocabulary, and it is sharper: Arabic 52%, Turkish 39%, Indonesian 36%, Portuguese 27%, Hindi 20%, Spanish 18%, English 9%, Japanese 2%. Arriving twice by separate roads is worth more than arriving once.
Japanese at 2% is the sharpest single figure in this book. Facing the same deaths, in the same years, Japanese speakers reach for God about one time in fifty.
An earlier version of this chapter said humour was the most common thing people reach for. That was a keyword counting the word laugh, which mostly caught people enjoying a video. Coded properly it is 0.3%, four cases in a thousand. The claim was wrong and is withdrawn. A companion guess, that humour is used to hide grief from others, found twelve people in 2,400 and is also withdrawn.
The eighth chapter

VIIIOf who is holding the pen

Sort every fragment not by where the speaker lives but by what had just happened to them, and a second axis appears, running underneath all the others. On one side are the people to whom life did something: a diagnosis arrived, a husband died, a child did not. On the other are the people who did something to their life: stopped drinking, left the country, walked out of the job, came out of prison. The same question, asked of both, comes back different.

44%Life happened to them, and they say God
27%They acted, and they say becoming themselves

Where life is done to a person, God is the answer nearly half the time, and everything else trails far behind. Where a person acts on their own life, God falls to 18%, while becoming yourself more than doubles and freedom appears from almost nowhere to take third place. Neither of these is a small wobble. Both are far beyond what chance would produce.

Meaning appears to depend on who is holding the pen. Handed a life you did not choose, people reach outward and upward. Having chosen it, they reach inward. The same person, in a different week, would likely answer differently, which is the least comfortable idea in this book.

The two gaps are large enough to be certain of, unlike most of the first chapter. Faith halves, and self-making doubles.
The acting side is a quarter the size of the other. The direction is not in doubt; the exact figures on that side will move.
The chorus

Fifty of them, speaking at once

The chapters above turn these people into percentages, which is useful and also a kind of theft. So here they are as themselves, in their own alphabets, each one under the thing that happened to them. Choose a situation and listen to what it does to the answer.

The colophon, in which the scribe admits everything

Every survey about meaning asks the question out loud, which is why surveys collect performances. This book never asked. It went to the places where the question asks itself: a hospice channel, a sobriety anniversary, an emigration diary, a faith coming apart. Then it gathered what people volunteered on their way past.

Four public sources. Comments beneath videos. A technical forum reaching back nineteen years. The question and answer sites of six religious and philosophical traditions. And interpretive writing about 1,429 novels, films and plays. Names were hashed on arrival and the originals were never kept. Nothing here quotes more than a breath of anyone.

No person read a million comments, and nobody should claim to have. Two machines did. A fast careless one went through everything and was often badly wrong: it reported that a fifth of these people deny life has any meaning at all. A slower, better machine re-read a sample of 16,800 one at a time, and the true figure turned out to be zero out of eight hundred. What was human here was the doubt. Someone noticed that videos about parenting were producing the answer family, and asked whether that was a discovery or a mirror. Someone asked whether the samples were large enough, and they were not. Someone asked whether the ranking was measuring culture or measuring which videos a search engine happened to serve, which is how the middle of the first chapter came to be marked unknown.

What all of it adds up to is not an answer. It is a shape. There is no single meaning of life in this text, and yet the way it varies is orderly. It bends with the tongue you speak, with the room you are standing in, and with whether life is happening to you or you are doing something to life. That bending is the finding.

The last chapter

Which you are writing

Every person in this book answered without being asked. You are being asked, which makes your answer a different kind of thing, and it will be kept separately for exactly that reason. Write it anyway. The page has been ruled and waiting.
